Fall Protection vs Working at Heights in Ontario: They Are Not the Same Course

In Ontario, fall protection training and the mandatory Working at Heights program are different things. Here is how to tell them apart and avoid a rejected card.

  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. Fall Protection vs Working at Heights

By A.RSM Training Centre · August 17, 2026 · 7 min read

Fall protection vs working at heights in Ontario is a distinction that costs workers time and money every year. A worker in Oshawa takes a harness course, arrives at a construction site in Newmarket, and is turned away because the site wants Working at Heights. Both courses have “heights” in their story, and both involve harnesses. They are not interchangeable.

What Working at Heights is

Working at Heights (WAH) is a specific mandatory training program for workers on Ontario construction projects. Under Regulation 213/91 section 26.2 and O. Reg. 297/13, a worker who may use a fall protection system on a construction project must complete a training program approved by the Chief Prevention Officer (CPO) of Ontario. The training must come from a provider that the CPO has approved, and it is valid for three years.

The program has a defined standard. A provider cannot design its own version and call it Working at Heights. The provider, the program and the completion record all need to trace back to the approval.

If your employer or site says “Working at Heights,” they mean the CPO-approved program from an approved provider, not a general harness course.

What a fall protection course is

A general fall protection course teaches the equipment and the reasoning: hazards of working at heights, how a harness fits, what an anchor point is, how a lanyard and a lifeline work, how a fall arrest system differs from a restraint or a guardrail, and how to inspect gear. That is what A.RSM Training Centre's Fall Protection course is: the school describes it as covering the hazards of working at heights. It is listed at $100.

It is useful, especially for industrial and maintenance work, for roofing prep, or for anyone whose employer wants baseline harness training. But it is not the CPO-approved Working at Heights program, and A.RSM Training Centre should not be read as an approved WAH provider. If you need WAH for a construction project, check the approved provider list before you pay.

How to tell which one you need

  1. Ask your employer or the general contractor in writing: “Do you require the CPO-approved Working at Heights program?”
  2. If you are on a construction project in Ontario and may use a fall protection system, the answer is very likely yes.
  3. If you work in an industrial establishment (a plant or warehouse) and your employer wants harness training for maintenance or roof access, a general fall protection course may be what they mean. Ask which standard they follow.
  4. If the answer is unclear, get it from the employer, not from the training provider.

Approved providers and fake cards

The Ministry of Labour publishes a list of approved Working at Heights training providers. Check that list, and check that the program is the one listed. Be careful with anyone who promises a same-day card for a bargain price with no verifiable approval number. Fraudulent Working at Heights cards have been an enforcement issue in Ontario, and a worker holding one can be removed from site, and the employer can face consequences. Before you pay, ask for the provider's approval information and confirm it against the government list.

After training, the provider issues a completion record. Keep it, and photograph it. Also, put the three-year expiry date on your phone.

What an employer should do

  • State in the job or contract documents which training is required.
  • Verify the training on arrival at site instead of after an incident.
  • Track expiry dates. Three years goes by faster than people think.
  • Keep fall protection equipment in inspected, working order, and make sure workers know how to inspect and store it.
  • Have a rescue plan. A worker hanging in a harness is in an emergency within minutes.

Can one course cover both?

Sometimes people take a general course first for skills and then the approved program for compliance. There is no shame in that. What you should avoid is paying for a course expecting it to satisfy a requirement it was never designed for. The right order is to find out the requirement first, then choose the course.

A practical example

Picture a roofing helper from Clarington who takes a harness course, then gets hired by a general contractor on a project. On the first morning the site supervisor asks for proof of the approved Working at Heights program. The helper does not have it. He loses a day of pay, and the contractor loses a worker. Now picture a maintenance technician in an Oshawa plant who needs to tie off to change a light fixture on a mezzanine. The employer asks for a general fall protection course and a documented site procedure. Two different workplaces, two different requirements. The lesson is simply to ask first, in writing, and buy the training that matches the answer.

Whichever course you take, ask to handle the equipment yourself. A harness that is adjusted wrongly can fail to hold a body properly even when the anchor is sound. You should leave able to put on a harness, adjust the leg and chest straps, check the stitching and hardware, and explain why a piece of gear that has arrested a fall must be taken out of service until inspected.

What to do next

Find out in writing whether your site requires the CPO-approved program. If what you need is the harness and fall protection fundamentals, call A.RSM Training Centre at (416) 297-5435 to ask what the course covers and how it is delivered. If you need the approved program, use the Ministry's list of approved providers.

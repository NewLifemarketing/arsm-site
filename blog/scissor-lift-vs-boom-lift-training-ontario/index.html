Scissor Lift vs Boom Lift Training in Ontario: Which Ticket Does Your Crew Need?

Scissor lifts, boom lifts and the new Ontario rule for elevating work platform training. What applies today, what changes on January 1, 2027, and what to ask.

By A.RSM Training Centre · September 7, 2026 · 7 min read

Scissor lift vs boom lift training in Ontario is a question construction supervisors in Vaughan and Markham ask every spring, usually when a new crew shows up and half of them have never been on an elevated platform. The short answer is that the class of machine matters, the rules for construction projects are changing on January 1, 2027, and neither a scissor lift nor a boom lift is something you let a worker figure out on the day.

Scissor lift or boom lift: the difference that matters

A scissor lift raises a flat platform straight up on crossed supports. It is generally used for work directly overhead on a level surface, and the platform is wide. A boom lift, whether telescopic or articulating, puts a smaller basket at the end of an arm that can reach up and out. Booms travel further, swing, and can throw the basket around if the machine is bumped or the operator makes a sudden control input.

Both are elevating work platforms. The hazards overlap: tip-over, falls from the platform, contact with overhead power lines, and crushing against a structure. But the controls, the stability limits, and the rescue procedures differ, and so does the training that makes sense.

What the regulation says today

On Ontario construction projects, Regulation 213/91 section 147 says a worker who operates an elevating work platform must, before using it for the first time, be given oral and written instruction on the operation and be trained to operate that class of elevating work platform. The important phrase is “that class.” Training on a scissor lift does not prove training on a boom.

Section 107 of the same regulation adds a rule that catches people out. No worker shall use as a work place a platform, bucket, basket, load, hook or sling that is capable of moving and that is supported by a fork-lift truck, front-end loader or similar machine. In plain terms: no pallet-and-cage on the forks. If you need to work at height, rent the right platform.

What changes on January 1, 2027

Ontario Regulation 117/26 comes into force on January 1, 2027 for construction projects. It sets out a training program for mobile elevating work platforms that includes theory and practical training, familiarization with the make and model, and a validity period of five years from successful completion.

There is a transition. If a worker completed training before the regulation comes into force that meets the requirements of section 147 for the group and type of equipment they use, they do not need to complete the new program until five years after that earlier training. As always, read the regulation itself or ask your safety advisor about the details for your project, since we are summarizing here.

The new rule turns “trained on that class” into a defined program with a practical component and a five-year clock. Start keeping the training date on file now.

Which ticket does your crew need?

  • If the crew will only use scissor lifts, a scissor lift course that includes practical time on a self-propelled platform is the fit.
  • If any of them will be in a boom, ask the provider directly whether the course covers boom-type platforms. Do not assume.
  • If you rent different makes, plan for a familiarization session on each make and model when it arrives on site. The new regulation expects this and it is good practice regardless.
  • If your crew works from a platform where a harness and lanyard are required, they also need fall protection training. Many employers and manufacturers require a harness in a boom basket, and site rules often do too.

The school describes the platform course as covering how to recognize and control hazards on self-propelled elevated work platforms. Fall protection is a separate question

Training on the platform tells you how to drive and set up the lift. Fall protection training is about the harness, the anchor point, and what happens to your body when you fall. A scissor lift with proper guardrails is often used without a harness, but a boom basket often requires one. Your employer's procedure decides. If a harness is needed, the fall protection course is the companion, not a replacement.

Inspection, ground and weather

Every day before use, the platform needs an inspection by someone trained to do it. Check the ground: soft fill on a new subdivision in Vaughan or a slope at a Markham site will change everything. Check the wind, the overhead wires, and the exclusion zone below. Remember the rule for rescue: someone on the ground must know how to lower the platform, and that plan needs to exist before anyone goes up.

Questions to ask a training provider or rental company

  1. Which classes of platform does the practical portion cover: scissor, boom, or both?
  2. Will the training meet the theory and practical requirements of O. Reg. 117/26 once it is in force, or only section 147 today?
  3. Does the record show the date, so we can track the five-year period?
  4. Who does the make-and-model familiarization on our site when the rental arrives?
  5. What is the rescue plan for a platform that will not lower?

Rental companies often provide a quick walk-through when they deliver a machine. That is helpful, but it is familiarization, not a substitute for training. Keep a signed record of both.

Also be careful about wording on your own paperwork. Say what the training covered, not what you assume it covered. If the record says scissor lift, do not send that worker into a boom basket.

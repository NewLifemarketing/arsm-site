Skid-Steer Training for Landscapers and Contractors in Durham and York Region

Skid-steer training for landscapers and contractors around Uxbridge, Port Perry, Stouffville and East Gwillimbury: what the rules say and what actually keeps operators safe.

By A.RSM Training Centre · August 10, 2026 · 7 min read

Skid-steer training for landscapers and contractors in Durham and York Region is a topic that tends to come up after a close call, not before. The machine is small, quick, and easy to hire. Around Uxbridge, Port Perry, Stouffville and East Gwillimbury, that means a lot of them show up on sites, farms and residential jobs with operators who learned on the job. This article covers what the rules say, and what makes the difference between a competent operator and a lucky one.

Is there a skid-steer licence in Ontario?

No. No Ontario regulation names skid-steer licences. What the law does say is that the machine must be operated by a competent person. In industrial establishments, Regulation 851 section 54 requires mobile equipment to be operated only by a competent person, or by a worker being instructed and accompanied by one. On construction projects, Regulation 213/91 section 96 says no worker shall operate a vehicle or powered machine unless he or she is competent to do so. Under the Occupational Health and Safety Act, the employer must give information, instruction and supervision.

So there is no government ticket. But the employer, and often the client, the insurer and the general contractor, will ask how you know the operator is competent. A training record answers that.

The law asks whether the operator is competent. A course record, plus supervised experience, is how you show it.

Why skid-steers hurt people

The school describes the skid-steer as a machine whose controls make it valuable and dangerous. That is accurate. Skid-steers steer by driving the wheels or tracks on each side at different speeds, they turn on the spot, and they lift loads out in front of the operator with a short wheelbase. The failure modes are well known:

  • Rollover: on slopes, when turning with the bucket raised, or when travelling with a load too high. Keep loads low, travel slowly, and go straight up and down slopes rather than across them.
  • Being struck or crushed by the lift arm: operators get hurt when they step out with the arms raised, or when a bystander is under a raised load. The lift arm restraint or lockout is there for a reason.
  • Running over or backing into people: the view to the rear is limited. Someone should be watching, and the operator should check.
  • Falling material: a raised bucket of loose material can slide back. Screens and canopies exist for this.
  • Ejection: a lap bar and a seatbelt keep you in the cab in a rollover. Fasten the belt every time.

Rollover protection and restraints

Skid-steers are built with a cab structure meant to protect the operator in a rollover (ROPS) and to guard against falling objects (FOPS). Those protections only work if the operator is inside and belted in. Never remove the door, disable the seat bar or the restraint interlocks, and never operate with a damaged cab. Reg 851 section 54 also requires a screen or canopy guard when the equipment is exposed to falling material.

Attachments change the machine

Landscapers and contractors run everything from pallet forks to augers, trenchers, grapples, brooms and brush cutters. Each attachment changes the balance, the reach, and the hazards:

  1. Confirm the attachment is designed for your machine and that the coupler is fully locked before you lift.
  2. Know the rated operating capacity with that attachment, not just with the bucket.
  3. Pallet forks raise the risk of a load sliding toward the operator.
  4. Augers and trenchers add underground-strike risk. Locates come first, digging second.
  5. Brush cutters and mowers throw debris. Keep bystanders well clear.

What a competent operator can show you

  • A walk-around inspection before each shift, including the restraint bar, seatbelt, lift arm lock and the coupler.
  • The habit of keeping the bucket low while travelling.
  • How to load a trailer safely, including ramp angles and tie-down points.
  • What to do if the machine starts to tip.
  • How to shut down and secure the machine, and how to exit safely.

Hiring and running a crew

If you own a small landscaping outfit, do not let a new hire run the machine on their first day without supervision. Reg 851 section 54 allows a worker to operate while being instructed and accompanied by a competent person. Use that time to teach them your sites and your attachments, and write down the date you signed them off.

Working around farms, lawns and soft ground

Rural Durham and northern York jobs bring their own hazards. Soft, wet ground after rain can bog a machine or let it slide on a slope. Old septic beds, buried tile drains and irrigation lines make digging unpredictable. Overhead wires cross many lanes and driveways, and a raised bucket does not need to touch a line to be dangerous. Before you start, walk the site, mark the slopes, note the wires, and decide where people will be standing. Then tell everyone who works nearby where they may stand and where they may not.

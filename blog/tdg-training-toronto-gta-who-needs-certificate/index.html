TDG Training in Toronto and the GTA: Who Needs a Certificate and How Long It Lasts

Who needs TDG training in Toronto and the GTA, who issues the certificate, how long it lasts by mode of transport, and how long to keep the records.

By A.RSM Training Centre · August 24, 2026 · 6 min read

TDG training in Toronto and the GTA matters most to people who never think of themselves as being in transportation: the shipper in a North York plant, the receiver in a Vaughan distribution centre, the dispatcher who books a pickup. If your work touches dangerous goods that are being shipped, handled for transport, or received, the federal rules on training apply to you. Here is who is covered, who signs the certificate, and how long it lasts.

What TDG is

TDG stands for Transportation of Dangerous Goods. The federal Transportation of Dangerous Goods Act and its Regulations, administered by Transport Canada, apply when dangerous goods are handled, offered for transport, or transported. Part 6 of the Regulations deals with training.

Who needs a TDG certificate

Part 6 says a person who handles, offers for transport, or transports dangerous goods, or who performs one of those functions on behalf of someone else, needs to be trained and hold a certificate. In practice that covers a wide range of roles:

  • Shippers and shipping clerks who prepare packages, mark them and complete shipping documents.
  • Receivers and dock workers who unload and handle dangerous goods.
  • Truck drivers moving dangerous goods on the road.
  • Dispatchers and coordinators who decide how goods move.
  • Warehouse staff who load, segregate or secure dangerous goods.
  • Supervisors who sign off on any of the above.

It is the function that counts, not the job title. A production supervisor who occasionally puts a drum on a truck is doing a TDG function.

The employer issues the certificate

This surprises people. Under Part 6, the employer issues the training certificate, not the training school. A training provider delivers instruction, and the employer confirms the person is adequately trained for the functions they perform and then signs the certificate. A wallet card from a school is useful evidence of instruction, but it is the employer's certificate that satisfies the regulation.

The certificate is the employer's document. A course gives you the knowledge, and the employer certifies that you can do the specific tasks in their workplace.

How long does TDG training last?

A TDG training certificate expires after 36 months for road, rail and vessel modes. For air transport, it expires after 24 months. The employer must keep a copy of the certificate and training records, and to keep them for two years after the expiry. Training is also required again sooner if the regulations change in a way that affects the person's duties, or if the functions they perform change.

What TDG training covers

Generally, it covers classification of dangerous goods into nine classes, how to read the Schedule and the shipping name, packaging and containers, marking and safety marks (labels and placards), shipping documents, and emergency response. For the person on a dock, the practical items are the hazard class diamonds, the UN numbers, what a compatible load looks like, and what to do in a release. The school describes its course as covering the requirements for shipping and handling dangerous goods.

Common mistakes we would look for

  • A certificate that was issued by a school but never signed by the employer.
  • A driver who is trained for road but is now asked to prepare an air shipment. Different mode, different rules.
  • Training records that cannot be found two years after expiry.
  • A new hire put on the dock before anyone checked whether they hold a current certificate.
  • An assumption that small quantities are exempt. Some exemptions exist, but the person applying them still needs to understand the rules.

A simple record-keeping routine

Keep one file per person: name, functions performed, date of training, who delivered it, the date the employer signed, the expiry date, and the mode of transport. Put the expiry on a shared calendar with a reminder at 60 days. When someone leaves, keep the file for two years after the expiry.

Small quantities, big misunderstandings

A common belief at Toronto and GTA shops is that a small shipment does not count. Some exemptions do exist, but you need to understand the rules to know whether your product and quantity qualify, which is training in itself. Another common belief is that the carrier handles everything. In fact, the person who offers goods for transport has duties around classification, packaging, marks and documents, whatever the carrier does afterwards. If you send lithium batteries, paint, aerosols, cleaning products or lab chemicals by courier, ask your supervisor whether anyone on site has TDG training, and if not, fix that before the next pickup.

One more point for employers: retrain when the job changes, not only when the date runs out. A receiver who starts preparing outbound shipments is performing new functions, and the certificate should reflect them. Keep the shipping documents from a few recent shipments on file too, since they show how the training is applied in practice. If a driver or clerk says they were never shown how to complete a shipping document, treat that as a training gap rather than a personal failing.

Warehouse Forklift Training for Mississauga and Brampton Employers: A Compliance Checklist

A working checklist for warehouse and distribution managers in Mississauga, Brampton and Caledon: what to document, what to train, and where classroom cards stop.

  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. Warehouse Forklift Training Checklist

By A.RSM Training Centre · September 14, 2026 · 6 min read

Warehouse forklift training for Mississauga and Brampton employers is not complicated in principle, but the paperwork is where most sites fall short. Between the distribution centres along the 401 and 407 corridors and the smaller operations in Caledon, the same gaps turn up. This checklist is the version a health and safety lead can use on a Monday morning.

Start with what the law asks of you

Ontario does not issue forklift licences. Regulation 851 (Industrial Establishments) says lifting devices and mobile equipment must be operated by a competent person, or by a worker being instructed and accompanied by one. The Occupational Health and Safety Act, section 25, requires the employer to provide information, instruction and supervision, and to appoint competent supervisors. The Ministry's guidance on powered lift trucks says the employer establishes operator competence and may issue certificates.

Read that carefully: the duty is yours. A card from a training school is good evidence. It is not a substitute for you being satisfied that this operator can safely drive this truck, in this building, with these loads.

Outside training is the start of competence. Your site orientation finishes it, and your records prove both.

The checklist

  1. List every powered truck on site by type: counterbalance, reach truck, order picker, walkie rider, pallet jack, and any attachments.
  2. List every operator against the types they drive. One card per truck type, not one card per person.
  3. Check that each operator has been trained on theory and evaluated on the machine, and note the date.
  4. Check the re-evaluation date. Most employers follow CSA B335, a voluntary standard, and re-evaluate at least every three years. That is industry practice rather than an Ontario statute.
  5. Complete a site-specific orientation: aisle widths, racking, pedestrian crossings, dock plates, trailer loading, speed limits, horn points, charging or fuelling areas.
  6. Confirm the daily pre-use inspection is done and recorded, and that a truck failing inspection is tagged out.
  7. Set rules for who may raise a person on a truck and what platform or safeguards are required. Reg 851 section 52 covers lift trucks raising workers.
  8. Keep records where a supervisor or inspector can find them in minutes.

Match the course to the truck

The most common mistake in a busy warehouse is assuming one card covers everything. It does not. A.RSM Training Centre lists separate courses for counterbalance forklift, reach truck, order picker and walkie rider, each at $150. They are separate because the machines are different.

  • Counterbalance: the classic sit-down truck for loading docks and yard work. Load sits ahead of the front axle, and stability depends on the load centre and the mast angle.
  • Reach truck: narrow aisle, high racking. The mast reaches forward and the operator often faces sideways. Spatial awareness at height is the hazard.
  • Order picker: the operator rises with the load to pick from the rack. Fall protection and restraint systems matter here.
  • Walkie rider: low-level pallet handling. It looks harmless, and it is where a lot of foot and pedestrian injuries happen.

Some of the course pages mention an extra $75 for practical training and wallet card creation. That is not confirmed for every course, so ask for a written all-in quote when you price a group.

The gap between a card and competence

Here is a scenario we see described in nearly every warehouse. A new hire arrives with a valid card. The supervisor is busy, so the new hire goes straight to the floor. Two weeks later a pallet clips a rack upright. Nobody trained the person on the aisle layout, and nobody was accompanying them. The card was fine. The orientation was missing.

Under Reg 851, a worker who has not yet been shown to be competent may operate the equipment while being instructed and accompanied by a competent person. Use that provision on purpose. Assign a buddy for the first shifts and write it down.

Group training and onsite options

One of the reviewers on the school's Google profile describes arranging onsite training for a company. We have not confirmed group rates, class sizes, or how onsite scheduling works, so ask directly about your headcount, truck types and shifts. If you have operators across several trucks, a mixed plan is common: send some to the Scarborough centre and arrange the rest separately.

What inspectors and insurers ask for

  • Proof that each operator was trained and evaluated on the type of truck they use.
  • The date of the last evaluation and the next one.
  • Evidence of a daily inspection routine.
  • Evidence that your site rules were communicated, not just posted.
  • Incident and near-miss records, and what changed afterwards.

Seasonal hires and agency workers

Peak season is when most warehouse incidents happen, because new people, temporary staff and agency workers arrive quickly and trucks run longer hours. Decide before the rush who will verify cards, who does the site orientation, and who accompanies new operators. Agency staff are still workers on your floor. Your duty to provide instruction and supervision does not disappear because someone else pays their wages. Ask the agency what training they can document, then run your own short orientation and practical check before anyone drives alone.

Finally, review your pedestrian plan. Most serious lift truck injuries involve people on foot. Marked walkways, mirrors, speed limits and a firm rule against phones on the floor cost little compared with one incident.

Transportation of Dangerous Goods, or TDG, is the federal law governing how dangerous goods are classified, packaged, labelled, documented and moved by road, rail, air and water in Canada. It covers everything from bulk fuel to the drum of solvent on a pallet at a dock. If you handle, offer for transport or transport dangerous goods, TDG applies to you. A product can be an ordinary workplace chemical and still be dangerous goods in transit.

The hazards are practical. A wrongly classified product gets the wrong placard. An incorrect or missing shipping document means responders do not know what they face. Damaged containment, unsecured drums or incompatible goods loaded together can leak, ignite or react in transit. The problems usually start with paperwork and packaging, not with the vehicle. Responders reading a shipping document at the roadside need to trust what it says, and mistakes there have consequences well beyond the driver.

The course covers the TDG Act and Regulations at working level: the nine classes, shipping documents, means of containment, safety marks such as labels and placards, emergency response and reporting releases. It is practical training for people who prepare or handle shipments, rather than a legal seminar. One fact matters: under TDG Regulations Part 6, the employer must issue the training certificate, and the person must be trained before performing the duties. Our course provides the training. The certificate your employer issues lasts 36 months for road, rail and vessel, and 24 months for air. We do not claim that our certificate replaces the one your employer issues.

What you will learn

  • What the TDG Act and Regulations require, and who is covered
  • The nine classes of dangerous goods and how to find a product's class
  • Shipping documents: required information and who signs
  • Means of containment: packaging selection and integrity
  • Safety marks: labels, placards, UN numbers and when each is required
  • Segregation and loading of mixed dangerous goods
  • Emergency response assistance plans and when they are required
  • Reporting releases and accidents
  • Worker and employer duties, including recordkeeping
  • Refresher cycle: 36 months for road, rail and vessel; 24 for air

Who this course is for

  • Shipping and receiving staff who handle labelled goods
  • Drivers and couriers carrying dangerous goods
  • Warehouse staff repackaging or staging hazardous products
  • Supervisors and dispatchers who prepare shipping documents
  • Employers building a TDG training record

TDG — common questions

Who issues my TDG certificate?

Under TDG Regulations Part 6, the employer issues the training certificate to the worker. A school provides the training. A.RSM's course gives you the knowledge; your employer signs and dates the certificate and keeps records for two years after it expires. Ask your employer whether they accept an outside course.

How long does TDG training last?

The certificate expires after 36 months for road, rail and vessel, and after 24 months for air. Retrain before it lapses, and sooner if you change jobs, if regulations change or you take on new duties. Employers must not let a worker perform TDG duties with an expired certificate.

Do I need TDG if I am a driver?

If you carry dangerous goods, yes. Drivers who transport dangerous goods must be trained and certified, and must have shipping documents in the cab. If you carry only small quantities that fall under exemptions, training may not be required. Ask your employer or check the regulations for your load.

Is TDG the same as WHMIS?

No. WHMIS is about hazardous products in the workplace: labels, data sheets and handling. TDG is about moving dangerous goods between locations. A warehouse worker who stores chemicals and loads trucks with them often needs both. Ask which applies to your role.
